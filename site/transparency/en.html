1. Can you see my login to my TV provider?

Your user name, password and server stay on your computer and go only to your provider – never to us.

What we can know: nothing of them. The app never sends them to dopeiptv.org, so there is nothing for us – or for anyone who took over our server – to read.

How it works

  1. You type them once, when you add a source.
  2. The app keeps them in its settings file, which only your own user account can read.
  3. Your provider gets them each time the app logs in or opens a channel – that is how it knows it is you.

Nothing else gets them by itself. From the moment the app first uses your login, its log writes your user name and password as *** wherever they turn up – in a stream's address, in an error from the video player, in a line written long after. An error report takes out the server, the user name and the password before you even see it (part 7). The update check carries none of it (part 3), and the list of every address in the app's code (part 4) shows where else the app goes. A Jellyfin or Emby password is sent once to that server, and only the token it answers with is kept.

Where your login does leave your computer, it is because you sent it there:

  • Settings sync, if you add Sources to what syncs – they are not among the defaults: locked on your computer with a key we never see (part 8).
  • A full backup holds it – a file you save where you choose, never sent anywhere.
  • A TV you cast to fetches the stream itself, so it gets the stream's address – and with many providers the login is part of that address.
  • A player you give the app's shared playlist (a Nerd setting) is sent on to the stream's own address the same way.

5. Could someone get a harmful update into my app?

The app you download is built in public from the version's tag in this code, signed, and it can tell when it has been changed.

An update is never used just because it arrived. Every release comes with a list of its files' checksums, signed with our own release key. The app checks the signature and the file's checksum before it installs anything. Someone who could change a file on GitHub, but does not hold our key, cannot get an update into your app. A copy built by someone else, without our key, takes no update at all.

  • Built in public. Every release is built by GitHub Actions from the version's tag in the code – not on our computers. The build's recipe is in the code, and the log of every build is open to anyone.
  • Signed. The release's files are listed with their checksums, and the list is signed with our release key. The app checks an update against it, and this website checks every file it hands out against it too, before it hands it out (below).
  • Not yet reproducible. The build's log and the signature show what was built, from which tag and where. They are not a byte-for-byte proof: the builds are not reproducible yet, so you cannot build the same bytes yourself and compare. Until they are, the open log and recipe are what you can check.
  • It checks itself. The app carries a signed list of its own files and checks them when it starts. A changed copy knows it is changed, and does not use our services: it looks for no updates here and sends no reports to us. Run straight from the code, it says so, and works as an official build.
  • A courtesy, not a lock. The check is made by the app itself, and what kind of build it is the app says about itself. A copy changed on purpose can change the check too, and anyone can write a request by hand. So none of the protection on this page rests on it – that is the encryption and the cleaning above. What it gives you is an app that knows when it is not ours, and says so.

6. Can the app keep nothing of what I watch?

While private viewing is on, nothing you play is kept on your computer or told to anyone – until you close the app.

What we can know: nothing of it. Private viewing is about what your own computer keeps and tells; we get nothing of what you watch either way (part 2).

How it works

  1. One switch in the app's memory – never saved, so it is off again when the app starts.
  2. Each place that keeps something – watch history, where you stopped – asks the switch itself before it writes.
  3. Each place that tells – Trakt, Simkl, Discord, your media server, the statistics, the log – asks it too.

The check sits in the stores themselves, not in the buttons that play something. So no part of the app can forget it: whichever way you start a film, the history refuses to write it. A playback that began in private stays private to its end – even if the stream reconnects after you turn private viewing off: it is not kept, not counted and not told to Trakt, Simkl, Discord or your media server.

7. What is in an error report?

An error report is cleaned on your computer of what the app recognizes as yours or as what you watch, you read all of it first – and nothing leaves at all until you press Send.

What we can know: what you choose to send – the report after its cleaning, which you read first – and an answer's place in the line, by a code only your computer has. Never your IP address in what we keep.

How it works

  1. The app writes the report and cleans it on your computer.
  2. You read all of it, change or delete anything, and decide whether to send it.
  3. Our server cleans it once more and keeps it without your IP address.

The cleaning works in two ways. First it looks for patterns: anything shaped like a web address – with or without http:// in front –, an IP or MAC address, an e-mail address, a password, token or login written as name=value, in quotes or as JSON, and a login header or cookie. Then it removes what the app knows is yours: the names, servers and logins of your sources, your channel names, the titles you have watched, your user name and your home folder. Each is replaced by a placeholder such as <private>, so the report still makes sense to us without saying who you are. The part that sends runs the patterns once more on exactly what goes, whichever way the text came, and sends it only over HTTPS to our own address – never after a redirect elsewhere.

If you ask for an answer, the app makes a random code that stays on your computer and sends only a hash of it – a one-way fingerprint we cannot turn back into the code. Later the app asks whether that hash has an answer. The hash is the same each time it asks, so our server can tell that the questions belong together, but not who asks. A reply you write to one of our answers is cleaned the same way as the report.

Our server keeps a report for 12 months and then removes it by itself – with every answer we wrote to it and every reply you sent to one. An answer we write to a whole problem at once (“fixed in 1.5.1”, for everyone who reported it) holds only our own words and stays until we remove it.

8. Can you read what I sync?

What you sync is locked on your computer before it is sent, with a key we never see – we keep it, but we cannot read it or tell whose it is.

What we can know: that a locked copy exists, how big it is and when it changes – not what is in it, nor whose it is.

How it works

  1. Your computer makes a random sync code – 256 bits, shown as 13 groups of four.
  2. From the code come two unrelated values: a slot (where the copy is kept) and a key (which locks it).
  3. Our server gets the slot and the locked copy. Never the code, never the key.

The two values come from the code through HKDF-SHA256, a standard way to make several keys from one secret: knowing the slot says nothing about the key. The copy is compressed and then encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305, one of the ciphers that protect HTTPS connections on the web. It also detects any change: a copy someone has tampered with does not open.

What our server could still do is hand back an earlier copy it kept. So every copy carries a counter, locked with the rest and one higher with every upload. A computer that has already seen a later copy notices, keeps its own newer values, puts its copy back and tells you so. Your other computers get the same code from you – typed or pasted – and so make the same slot and key.

The request carries the app's name and version, whether it is an official build, the slot and the locked copy. No account, no e-mail address, no name. It goes only over HTTPS, and never after a redirect elsewhere. The web server does not log sync requests at all, and a copy no computer has read or written for 180 days is deleted.

9. Can you see what we watch together?

Who is in the room and what you watch go between your computers locked – our server only passes on messages it cannot open.

What we can know: that a room is in use, how many are in it and when they send – not who they are, what they watch or what they say.

How it works

  1. The room code – ten characters you share with your friends – stays with the people in the room.
  2. From the code each computer makes the same room name and the same key.
  3. Our server passes the locked messages on, keeps them only in memory, and forgets the room an hour after the last request.

Ten characters are quick to type, but on their own they would be quicker to guess than a key should be. So the code is first stretched with PBKDF2 – 600,000 rounds of SHA-256, so trying every code against one room would keep a graphics card busy for centuries – and then turned into a room name and a key with HKDF, as for sync. A code of eight characters from an older version still joins its room. Each message – who joined, what plays, play, pause and seek – is encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305 and padded to one of four fixed sizes, so its length says little about what is in it. 10. Does this website track me?

We count visits without cookies, without outside services – and without your IP address ever being written down, not even in a log.

What we can know: that someone visited, from which country and which pages – for 14 days as a visit of that day alone, after that only as counts. Your IP address is never kept: it passes the server's memory for a minute at most.

How it works

  1. The web server writes nothing: it hands each page view straight to a small service of ours, over a socket on the same machine – with the IP address, and of the address you asked for only the page, never what a link added to it. The front server before it keeps no log of requests.
  2. Within a minute the service counts it, in its memory: the address becomes a country (looked up on our own server) and a visitor mark, and is forgotten. No log, no file and no backup ever holds it.
  3. What is written is the visit without the address and without the mark: its visitor named for that day alone, its time to the half hour, the kind of screen, never its size.
  4. After 14 days – never more, whatever the server's settings say (VISITS_DAYS_MAX) – the day's visits are deleted, every one. After every run the service looks for anything that should be gone and deletes it again; what it cannot delete it says in its log and at the top of the admin page until it is gone (left_behind, sweep_left). What stays is counts: how many visits, pages and downloads, and lists that name no country, language, version, site or tag fewer than five visitors had that day – the rest is "other", and no list adds days up again under a name.

The visitor mark is a keyed hash: the IP address and the browser's name run through HMAC-SHA256 with a key of the month, cut to twelve characters. The key is kept only in memory – in the service's own, or in a file on a file system held in memory (tmpfs) so that a restart of the service keeps it; never on a disk, so never in a backup – and is forgotten after two months, or at once when the computer starts again. What the system moves out of memory goes to swap encrypted with a key of its own that a restart forgets: the service asks the system (swap_state) and the admin page warns when swap is not encrypted, or when the system cannot say. Within a month the same visitor gets the same mark, so we can count unique and returning visitors – but the mark itself is never written down. A visit kept carries two things in its place: a name for its visitor that holds for that day alone – nothing kept ties a visitor's visits of two days together – and the mark sealed with the month's key, random to anyone without it. Only the running service can open it, while it holds the key: so that a visit can go on after a restart.

How many different visitors came over several days is kept as sketches: a field of 131,072 bits for each day, in which each visitor sets two – which two only the key says. Put together they count the visitors of a week or a month within a few in a thousand; they hold no mark, no list and no order. A visitor sets the same two bits on every day of its month, so two quiet days' sketches side by side could hint that someone came on both – which is why they are kept only where the key is: in the service's memory or beside the key on a file system in memory, never on a disk or in a backup. After the month that follows, only the numbers stay.

How long each part is kept: your IP address – never; a minute in memory at the most. Each visit in detail – of that day alone, its time to the half hour, the browser's main version, the kind of screen, the page's language and the browser's without its country, the site that linked by its registered name – 14 days, then deleted; no row of a visit is kept after that, not even an anonymous one. The sketches, in memory alone, until the month after has ended, then only the number of different visitors in that month and its weeks. The day's counts for good – per way in (search, social…) how many visits read on, downloaded or opened the support dialog, too – with no country, language, version, site or tag fewer than five visitors had that day. A payment is never noted on a visit – a tip counts only in the day's total – so a visit cannot be tied to the payment service's record of who paid.

11. What do the servers behind dopeiptv.org see and write down?

Your visit passes two web servers and a small service, and none of their logs keeps your address as a rule: the statistics' lines go straight to the service's memory. Here is every log there is, and the settings that decide it.

What we can know: the pages you ask for – your IP address only while the service counts them in memory, a minute at the most – and nothing at all of what the app's services carry.

How it works

  1. The front server, a TLS proxy in a jail of its own, holds the site's certificate and answers your encrypted connection. It passes the request on with your address in a header it sets itself, X-Real-IP – whatever your browser sent under that name is replaced.
  2. The content server, on a port of its own, serves the pages and files – all made in advance, every quarter of an hour; no PHP, no program run for a page – and hands the statistics' lines to the service, writing none of them down.
  3. A small service in Python (donate.py) answers what is not a file: a tip and its supporter key, feedback and error reports, settings sync, Watch together and the admin page. It listens on a Unix socket, not on the network, so from outside it is reached only through the content server, on the paths that server hands it – and on the machine only the content server and the service itself can connect to it.

A jail is FreeBSD's way of walling parts of one machine off from each other, each with its own files, users and programs. The content server and the service run in a jail of their own on the server, which holds nothing else – just them and the scheduled job that makes the pages and counts the downloads. The service runs as a user of its own, which no other program on the machine shares but that job, and what any of them deletes is gone from the server's backups within two days as well.

What may reach them. A firewall on the server lets into that jail only the front server, and only on that one port. Out of it, the jail may only look up names and open https connections – which the service and the scheduled job use for Stripe, GitHub, the browsers' push services for the admins' notifications, and the country database's download. nginx's own allow list, which the content server will not even start without, is a second lock behind the firewall.

What each one sees. The front server has to see everything to answer: your address, the address you ask for, what your browser sends with it, and a request's body – a report, a sync copy (already locked by the app). The content server gets the same request from it as plain HTTP, your address in X-Real-IP, and believes that header – which is why its port is kept to the front server alone, by the firewall and again by nginx. The service gets only the requests for its own paths, with the same header; it keeps your address only in memory, for its limits (part 7), and its own log says what went wrong, never from which address.

What is written down. None of the front server's servers for the site – dopeiptv.org, www.dopeiptv.org, plain http:// and the old iptv.dope.rs – keeps a log of requests, and each writes an error only at the level of what stops nginx itself (crit): a refused or broken request would otherwise be written there with its address. The content server is the same as a whole: it writes the statistics' lines to no file, but hands them to the service over its socket – and keeps one small log for the public download counter:

  • The statistics' lines: the front page, the help page and /hit, the signal a page sends once it is shown (part 10). A line each: your address, the time, the method, the page's path – never what a link added after it, a ?fbclid= or a ?utm_… –, for /hit only the fields the signal sends, the status, the page that linked here without its query or fragment, the browser's name and the page's language – the browser's name cut at 512 characters, the address asked for at 1,024, so a line fits one message on the socket. A download goes the same way. In the service's memory they are counted within a minute and gone; no file holds them.
  • The download counter: /dl.php, which every download link and the app's own update go through: the method, the file's name and the protocol, the answer, and whether a browser, the app, a robot or nothing asked – no address, no time, no browser's name.

Nothing else: no other page, no cookie, nothing a request carries in its body, nothing of sync, Watch together, feedback, tips or the admin page. A browser that has opened the admin page is left out of both. The counter's log is turned over at midnight, and only the day before is kept – two days at the most.

Every log there is. None of them keeps your address as a rule. All the logs on the server, what each holds, how long it is kept and who can read it:

  • iptv.dope.rs-dl-counts.log – the download counter's above, with no address: two days at the most. Readable by nginx's own user and the user that counts the downloads, no one else (mode 640).
  • error.log, one for each web server – only what stops nginx (crit), which is rare. Such a line names the request it happened on: on the front server with your address, on the content server with the front server's. Both are turned over every night with the six days before kept – a week at the most –, then deleted. The front server's is shared with the other sites behind it. Readable by nginx alone (600).
  • iptv-donate.log – the service's: when it starts and how it is set up; a fault by its kind and the file and line it happened at, never its words; why Stripe refused a payment, in Stripe's own codes; a push notification that failed; a sign-in to the admin page refused and why – never what was typed, never from where; and once a day how many sync copies, Watch together rooms, reports and refused modified builds came and went – numbers only; and the statistics' own lines: when the country database was fetched, or why not, how many lines could not be read – a number, never the line. No address, no request, nothing you sent. Five old copies of a megabyte each at the most: at its pace months, perhaps years. The service's alone (600).
  • iptv-sync.log – the job that mirrors each release from GitHub and makes the pages, nothing to do with settings sync: which release, which files were fetched, how many downloads were counted – a number –, how many pages were made. Nothing of a visit. Kept like the service's (600).

Beside them only FreeBSD's own system logs, which hold nothing of a request.

What guards them. Every way in to the service has a limit on the content server, counted by the address the front server set: 10 requests a minute for the tip dialog, the supporter key and feedback, 30 for sync, 120 for /hit and for downloads, 180 for Watch together, 20 for signing in, and on the admin page 30 for a browser without a session and 600 for any one address – each with a burst on top. Past it nginx answers 429, “slow down”. The front server limits connections and counts the pages against a generous allowance, and lets at most eight sync copies in at once from one address. nginx keeps these counts in memory, never on disk. Every way in also has a largest body – 1 KB for a tip, the supporter key and /hit; 4 KB for Watch together and asking for answers; 256 KB for a report; 3 MB for a sync copy; 64 KB for the rest – and nginx holds a body up to that size in memory, so nothing you send lies in a temporary file on disk, in either server. No answer does either: one larger than nginx's buffers – a sync copy can be 2 MB – is passed on as it comes.

What every answer carries. The front server adds the same security headers to every answer, a “not found” or an error too. Its content security policy lets a page load only what comes from dopeiptv.org itself, pictures written into the page (data:), and – for the tip dialog – Stripe's form and Google Pay's; no script or style written into a page runs (there is no 'unsafe-inline'), no plugin either, no <base> tag can change where a page's links point, no form can be sent anywhere, and no other site can show a page in a frame. Then: https only, for a year, the names under it too; a file is the type it is sent as, never guessed; only the site's name goes to the next site you open – nothing at all from the admin pages, the support page and the way back from Trakt; camera, microphone, location, screen capture, USB and the like are off for every page and frame; the admin pages open in a window of their own, and they and the services' answers are never loaded into another site's page. No answer names nginx's version. The one cookie the site gives a visitor is the language, and only when you pick one: for a year, sent only over https, and not along when another site's page posts here or loads a part of it. No name that starts with a dot is served – a forgotten .git folder or .env file would not be – but /.well-known/, where security.txt says how to tell us about a security problem: privately, through GitHub. And the content server follows no symbolic link in the site's folder, so a link left there by mistake cannot hand out a file from outside it.

What it does not protect against

  • To answer at all, the front server and the content server see your address and your request as they pass – in memory, for that moment. Whoever controls the machine could read them there, or turn the logs on.
  • From the front server to the content server a request goes as plain HTTP, between two jails on the same machine: it never crosses a network, but it is not encrypted there.
  • An error log kept for what stops nginx is empty by habit, not by promise: nginx files some failures there – a TLS handshake it cannot make sense of, a worker out of open files – and such a line names the request it happened on and, on the front server, your address. Both error logs are turned over every night and kept a week at the most.
  • This is configuration, and from outside you cannot see that the server runs it. What runs is what the owner copies to the server by hand, as DEPLOY.md says step by step. The headers, the “not found” for a name with a dot and security.txt you can check (above); the logs you cannot.
  • Some of the front server's settings are not in the repository, because its main file serves other sites too: the allowance for pages and the connection limit, its TLS versions and ciphers, and the policy's row itself (csp.snippet.txt is its copy). Test its TLS yourself with any TLS checker.
  • The content server believes the address it is told in X-Real-IP. That only the front server can reach its port rests on the server's firewall and on a small file with the front server's address – neither in the repository, since they hold the server's addresses. nginx will not start without the file, but that the firewall and the file say what we write here you have to take on trust. The few addresses that only send you on elsewhere, or answer “not found”, answer before nginx asks the file – that is all they give.
  • The service runs as the same user that makes the pages: a flaw in it that let someone run code could change the site. Who is signed in to the admin page it does not take from nginx: it checks the signed session itself.
  • The two logs with your address can be read by nginx's own user as well as by the statistics' – nginx makes a log its own when it starts on a new one; in that jail only nginx runs as that user.
  • The limits count addresses. People who share one – a phone network, a school – share its limits, and someone with many addresses gets many.
