1. Can you see my login to my TV provider?

Your user name, password and server stay on your computer and go only to your provider – never to us.

What we can know: nothing of them. The app never sends them to dopeiptv.org, so there is nothing for us – or for anyone who took over our server – to read.

How it works
  1. You type them once, when you add a source.
  2. The app keeps them in its settings file, which only your own user account can read.
  3. Your provider gets them each time the app logs in or opens a channel – that is how it knows it is you.

Nothing else gets them by itself. From the moment the app first uses your login, its log writes your user name and password as *** wherever they turn up – in a stream's address, in an error from the video player, in a line written long after. An error report takes out the server, the user name and the password before you even see it (part 7). The update check carries none of it (part 3), and the list of every address in the app's code (part 4) shows where else the app goes. A Jellyfin or Emby password is sent once to that server, and only the token it answers with is kept.

Where your login does leave your computer, it is because you sent it there:

  • Settings sync, if you add Sources to what syncs – they are not among the defaults: locked on your computer with a key we never see (part 8).
  • A full backup holds it – a file you save where you choose, never sent anywhere: plain text, unless you give it a password, which encrypts the whole file.
  • A TV you cast to fetches the stream itself, so it gets the stream's address – and with many providers the login is part of that address.
  • A player you give the app's shared playlist (a Nerd setting) is sent on to the stream's own address the same way.

5. Could someone get a harmful update into my app?

The app you download is built in public from the version's tag in this code, signed, and it can tell when it has been changed.

How it works

An update is never used just because it arrived. Every release comes with a list of its files' checksums, signed with our own release key. The app checks the signature and the file's checksum before it installs anything. Someone who could change a file on GitHub, but does not hold our key, cannot get an update into your app. A copy built by someone else, without our key, takes no update at all.

  • Built in public. Every release is built by GitHub Actions from the version's tag in the code – not on our computers. The build's recipe is in the code, and the log of every build is open to anyone.
  • Signed. The release's files are listed with their checksums, and the list is signed with our release key. The app checks an update against it, and this website checks every file it hands out against it too, before it hands it out (below).
  • Checked before it is signed. Every package a release ships is looked up against the known vulnerabilities first; an audit that finds one, or cannot finish, stops the release before it is signed. And yt-dlp, which the app can download for YouTube and the like, is only the release whose checksums the build checked against yt-dlp's own signature – and, from version 1.6.11, the copy on your computer is checked against them again before it runs, once each time the app starts and whenever the file changes.
  • It checks itself. The app carries a signed list of its own files and checks them when it starts. A changed copy knows it is changed, and does not use our services: it looks for no updates here and sends no reports to us. Run straight from the code, it says so, and works as an official build.

6. Can the app keep nothing of what I watch?

While private viewing is on, nothing you play is kept or told to anyone, and nothing of it is written to a disk unencrypted – until you close the app.

What we can know: nothing of it. Private viewing is about what your own computer keeps and tells; we get nothing of what you watch either way (part 2).

How it works
  1. One switch in the app's memory – never saved, so it is off again when the app starts.
  2. Each place that keeps something – watch history, where you stopped – asks the switch itself before it writes.
  3. Each place that tells – Trakt, Simkl, Discord, your media server, the statistics, the log – asks it too.
  4. What must be written while it plays – the rewind buffer, a cast the app converts for the TV, a subtitle you fetch – goes only to memory that cannot reach a disk unencrypted, or is not made at all. From version 1.6.11, a subtitle search opened while it plays asks OpenSubtitles nothing until you press Search.

The check sits in the stores themselves, not in the buttons that play something. So no part of the app can forget it: whichever way you start a film, the history refuses to write it. A playback that began in private stays private to its end – even if the stream reconnects after you turn private viewing off: it is not kept, not counted and not told to Trakt, Simkl, Discord or your media server.

Memory counts only when it really is memory: on Linux, a folder of your own on a file system in memory (tmpfs), and only when what the system moves out of memory cannot be read from a disk afterwards – no swap, zram with no disk behind it, or encrypted swap. With encrypted swap the system may move the buffer to the disk, then, but only encrypted. Where there is no such memory – on macOS and Windows, or with swap on a plain disk – a private channel plays without the rewind buffer, the app says why a cast that needs converting cannot be made, and a subtitle reaches the player without becoming a file. Turn private viewing on while a channel plays from a buffer on the disk, and it is opened again the private way – as soon as the app gets to it, after what is already under way in the window; a cast being converted on the disk stops within a second and is sent again. A recording while you watch privately is asked about first.

Private viewing counts from when you turn it on. A scrobble already begun at Trakt or Simkl is let go without being ended, so how far you got is not reported and it does not go into the history there; your media server hears where you were when you turned it on, and nothing more. Events not yet sent to Home Assistant or a webhook that name what plays are taken back.

7. What is in an error report?

An error report is cleaned on your computer of what the app recognizes as yours or as what you watch, you read all of its text first – and nothing leaves at all until you press Send.

What we can know: what you choose to send – the report after its cleaning, which you read first, with the app's version and your system (Linux, Windows or macOS) – and an answer's place in the line, by a code only your computer has. Never your IP address in what we keep.

How it works
  1. The app writes the report and cleans it on your computer.
  2. You read all of it, change or delete anything, and decide whether to send it.
  3. Our server cleans it once more and keeps it without your IP address.

The cleaning works in two ways. First it looks for patterns: anything shaped like a web address – with or without http:// in front –, an IP or MAC address, an e-mail address, a phone number, a password, token or login written as name=value, in quotes, as JSON or in a sentence such as “password: …”, a token on its own (a JWT, a key with a service's prefix, a long random string), and a login header or cookie. Then it removes what the app knows is yours: the names, servers and logins of your sources, your channel names, the titles you have watched, your user name and any account's home folder. Each is replaced by a placeholder such as <private>, so the report still makes sense to us without saying who you are. From version 1.6.11 a name is replaced only where it stands as a word of its own – hidden inside another word, the placeholder told what it was (“not st<private>d yet” with a channel called Arte) – and a login wherever it stands, together with the whole word around it. The part that sends runs the patterns once more on exactly what goes, whichever way the text came, and sends it only over HTTPS to our own address – never after a redirect elsewhere.

If you ask for an answer, the app makes a random code that stays on your computer and sends only a hash of it – a one-way fingerprint we cannot turn back into the code. Later the app asks whether that hash has an answer. The hash is the same each time it asks, so our server can tell that the questions belong together, but not who asks. When several of your reports wait for answers, the app asks for all their hashes in one request – so the server can tell they come from one computer; it keeps nothing of the asking. It asks only while you have asked for answers, for 30 days, and never with the report itself. A reply you write to one of our answers is cleaned the same way as the report.

8. Can you read what I sync?

What you sync is locked on your computer before it is sent, with a key we never see – we keep it, but we cannot read it or tell whose it is.

What we can know: that a locked copy exists, how big it is and when it is fetched or changed – and, as any server does, from which IP address – not what is in it, and nothing that says who you are.

How it works
  1. Your computer makes a random sync code – 256 bits, shown as 13 groups of four.
  2. From the code come two unrelated values: a slot (where the copy is kept) and a key (which locks it).
  3. Our server gets the slot and the locked copy. Never the code, never the key.

The two values come from the code through HKDF-SHA256, a standard way to make several keys from one secret: knowing the slot says nothing about the key. The copy is compressed and then encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305, one of the ciphers that protect HTTPS connections on the web. It also detects any change: a copy someone has tampered with does not open.

A third value comes from the code the same way: a password for the slot. Our server keeps only a hash of it, and once a copy is written nobody can read, change or delete that slot without it – knowing a slot is not enough.

What our server could still do is hand back an earlier copy it kept. So every copy carries a counter, locked with the rest and one higher with every upload. A computer that has already seen a later copy notices, keeps its own newer values, puts its copy back and tells you so. Your other computers get the same code from you – typed or pasted – and so make the same slot and key. A playlist's connection settings that a copy brings – a proxy, headers, a rewrite rule and the like – are never put in place unasked: the app shows them, and you choose.

The request carries the app's name and version, whether it is an official build, the slot, its password and the locked copy. No account, no e-mail address, no name. It goes only over HTTPS, and never after a redirect elsewhere. The web server does not log sync requests at all, and a copy no computer has read or written for 180 days is deleted.

9. Can you see what we watch together?

Who is in the room and what you watch go between your computers locked – our server only passes on messages it cannot open.

What we can know: that a room is in use, how many are in it and when they send – not who they are, what they watch or what they say.

How it works
  1. The room code – ten characters you share with your friends – stays with the people in the room.
  2. From the code each computer makes the same room name and the same key.
  3. Our server passes the locked messages on, keeps them only in memory, and forgets the room an hour after the last request.

Ten characters are quick to type, but on their own they would be quicker to guess than a key should be. So the code is first stretched with PBKDF2 – 600,000 rounds of SHA-256, so trying every code against one room would keep a graphics card busy for centuries – and then turned into a room name and a key with HKDF, as for sync. Each message – who joined, what plays, play, pause and seek – is encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305 and padded to one of four fixed sizes, so its length says little about what is in it. Each person gets a key of their own for each room (Ed25519) and is known in it by a hash of the key's public half: every message is signed with that key, so nobody – the relay included – can speak as someone else, and a message without a signature is left out. Each message also carries a count, remembered for the rest of the app's run: one passed on twice is used once, and one from well before you joined, or far from the relay's time, is not acted on. A code of eight characters from 1.6.7 no longer joins: the app says so, and a new room gives one of ten. The app talks to the relay only over HTTPS, and never follows a redirect elsewhere.

10. Does this website track me?

We count visits without cookies, without outside services – and without your IP address ever being written down, not even in a log.

What we can know: that someone visited – from which country, with which browser and system, which pages and for how long, what was downloaded and which site linked here – for 14 days as a visit, after that only as counts. Your IP address is never kept: it passes the server's memory for a minute at most.

How it works
  1. The web server writes nothing: it hands each page view straight to a small service – with the IP address, and of the address you asked for only the page, never what a link added to it.
  2. Within a minute the service counts it, in its memory: the address becomes a country (looked up on our own server) and a visitor mark, and is forgotten. No log, no file and no backup ever holds it.
  3. What is written is the visit without the address and with the mark only sealed: its visitor named for that day, its time to the half hour, the kind of screen, never its size.
  4. After 14 days – never more – the day's visits are deleted, every one, and the service checks that they really are gone. What stays is counts: how many visits, pages and downloads, and lists that name no country, language, version, site or tag fewer than five visitors had that day – the rest is "other", and no list adds days up again under a name.

The visitor mark is a keyed hash: the IP address and the browser's name run through HMAC-SHA256 with a key for the month. The key is only in memory, never on a disk or in a backup, and is forgotten after two months. Within a month the same visitor gets the same mark, so we can count unique and returning visitors. A visit kept carries the mark only sealed with the month's key: to someone with a copy of the disks it is random characters, and nothing else in the visit ties two days together. But the service itself can open it while the key is there – that is how it counts a visitor who comes back – and so can tell that two of the visits kept were one visitor's. Once the key is forgotten, after two months, no one can.

How long each part is kept: your IP address – never; a minute in memory at the most. Each visit in detail – the country, the browser and its main version, the system and the kind of device and screen, the page's language and the browser's without its country, the pages and the time on them, downloads and what was clicked, the site that linked by its registered name, its time to the half hour and what happened in it to ten seconds – 14 days, then deleted; no row of a visit is kept after that, not even an anonymous one. The count of different visitors – sketches that hold no mark, in memory alone – until the month after has ended, then only the number of different visitors in that month and its weeks. The day's counts for good – per way in (search, social…) how many visits read on, downloaded or opened the support dialog, too – with no country, language, version, site or tag fewer than five visitors had that day. A payment is never noted on a visit – a tip counts only in the day's total – so a visit cannot be tied to the payment service's record of who paid.

11. What do the servers behind dopeiptv.org see and write down?

Our servers keep no log of your visits, and your IP address is never written down as a rule.

What we can know: the pages you ask for – your IP address only in memory while they are counted, a minute at the most – and nothing at all of what the app's services carry.

How it works

The only things written down:

  • iptv.dope.rs-dl-counts.log – how many download which file; no address, no time. Two days at the most.
  • error.log – only faults that stop the web server, which is rare. Such a line can hold your address. A week at the most.
  • iptv-service.log and iptv-sync.log – the services' own, on how they are doing: no addresses and nothing you sent.

What is deleted is gone from the server's backups within two days. Want to know more about how the servers are set up? Ask us.

What it does not protect against 3
  • To answer at all, the servers see your address and your request while they answer – in memory. Whoever took over the machine could read them there.
  • An error log can now and then hold your address; it is kept a week at the most.
  • This is our configuration – that the server runs it you have to take on trust.
